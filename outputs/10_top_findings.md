# Top 5 findings (ranked)

Ranked by how strong the evidence is, how surprising it is, and how directly the office can act on it.
Sample: 336 respondents (273 students, 63 staff) at two festivals (Campus A n=177, Campus B n=159).
Staff and students answered different questions, so each finding is about one group.

---

## 1. Most staff don't know the Reuse Teams group exists, and the people asking for help are the ones who haven't heard of it

**Evidence**
- 60% of staff (37 of 62) are not aware of the group; 27% have used it. A third of those who know it (8 of 25) never used it.
- 56% (34 of 61) name "lack of awareness of reuse/sharing options" as their top barrier; 36% (22) say it is hard to find items or who has them.
- 11 of 21 staff who answered the help question asked for help or more information; 6 of those 11 had never heard of the group.

**Why it matters:** the group is built to solve the barrier staff name most, but most staff never reach it.

**Suggested action:** contact the staff who left a request (their emails were removed here; use the original file); add a short "what the group offers and how to join" message to departmental channels; track awareness again next year.

**Caveat:** small group (62 staff; only 21 at Campus B). Percentages carry margins of about +/-12 points. Festival-goers chose to answer, so awareness in the whole staff population is probably lower, not higher.

---

## 2. Three-quarters of students want at least one repair-or-share program that is not on the existing programs list

**Evidence**
- 76% of students (204 of 270; 95% range 70-80%) chose at least one of: fix-it clinics (47%), sharing shelf (44%), digital sharing platform (33%).
- 34% want two or more; 39 students want all three. Same pattern at both campuses (72% and 79%; difference not significant).

**Why it matters:** this is the clearest sign of demand for something new, and it comes from a large sample.

**Suggested action:** pilot one low-cost option (for example a sharing shelf or a single fix-it clinic day) and count attendance.

**Caveat:** confirm none of these already exists; if one does, this becomes a visibility finding. Ticking "want" is cheap, so this measures interest, not future use. Students had a stamp-card incentive.

---

## 3. Much of the "demand" is for things students already know about, so visibility and capacity matter as much as new programs

**Evidence**
- Bottle filling stations: 44% want more, 63% recognize them as existing; 72% of those who want them already recognize them (paired test p<0.001).
- Clothing swaps: 44% want them, 55% recognize them; 67% of wanters already recognize them (p=0.007).
- Reusable containers (top request, 58%): 83% of those asking also recognize the reuse program (assumes that program covers containers).

**Why it matters:** if people ask for what exists, the fix may be more locations, more events, or clearer signage, and not new programs.

**Suggested action:** add location/hours signage for existing programs; ask in next year's survey whether students have used each program.

**Caveat:** the survey cannot tell "wants more of it" from "didn't realize it exists". The reusable-containers link is an assumption to confirm with the office.

---

## 4. Interest in learning more is broad and cannot be predicted, so outreach should go to everyone

**Evidence**
- 68% of students (180 of 263) want to learn more. Across five predictors (campus, programs recognized, programs wanted, two belief items) no predictor is clearly related; the model is no better than always guessing "yes" (68% vs. 68%; AUC 0.60).
- Interested students know no more programs than others (4.1 vs. 4.0 of 8; p=0.78); half of the interested recognize 3 or fewer.

**Why it matters:** there is no "type of student" to target; the interested audience is large and mostly under-informed.

**Suggested action:** put basic program information in general channels (orientation, residence halls, event tables) instead of targeting subgroups.

**Caveat:** "no relationship found" is not proof of none; small effects (under about 1.5x in odds) could be hidden. "Yes" is a cheap answer at a festival.

---

## 5. Students rate recycling as high-impact as reusables, and rate reducing the least

**Evidence**
- 77% picked recycling and 77% picked reusables as high-impact; composting 50%; reducing printing paper (the only "reduce" option) 36%.
- 28% (77 students) picked all four options.

**Why it matters:** recycling is not reduction or reuse; the office's message may need to separate "reduce and reuse first".

**Suggested action:** test a short message on the reduce-reuse-recycle order at events and re-measure.

**Caveat:** weakest measure here: more than a quarter selected everything, "reduce" has only one option, and the question asks for beliefs, not behavior.

## Also worth saying (not ranked)

- **The two festivals look alike.** None of 23 campus comparisons survive correction for multiple testing; combine them for the deck.
- **Staff at Campus B are under-represented** (13% of that sample vs. 23% of the school); staff findings speak mostly for Campus A.
- **Paper is the top item staff say could be reduced** (11 of 38 text answers).
- **No question asks about actual behavior** (what students do); the survey measures awareness, belief and interest.
